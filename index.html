Study

What the project looks at

Most people know they should back up. Fewer do it on a schedule, and fewer still have ever restored a file to check it works.

Habits

How often people back up, which methods they use, and which kinds of data they protect first.

Barriers

What stops people: cost, time, not knowing how, or assuming nothing will go wrong.

Awareness

Whether people know the difference between sync, copy, and backup, and whether they have tested a recovery.

Objectives

  • Describe the backup methods people use today.
  • Find where habits are weakest: frequency, separation of copies, or restore testing.
  • Turn the findings into a guide short enough that people follow it.

Why backups matter

Hardware fails

Drives wear out, phones are dropped, laptops are stolen. A single copy is one accident away from gone.

People make mistakes

Files get deleted or overwritten. A backup with version history lets you go back.

Malicious software

Ransomware encrypts files in place. A copy it cannot reach, offline or off-site, is the way out.

Backup Guide

Five steps, in order

Do these once, then let a schedule do the repeating.

  1. Identify important dataList files that are hard, costly, or impossible to recreate: family photos, thesis drafts, tax records, project source files.
  2. Choose a backup locationPick an external drive, cloud storage, or both. Match the medium to the size and how often the data changes.
  3. Back up on a scheduleTurn on the built-in tool or a sync-and-version service. A weekly run beats a yearly good intention.
  4. Keep a separate copyA backup beside the original shares its fire, flood, and theft. Keep at least one copy somewhere else.
  5. Test a restoreEvery few months, restore one file to a different folder and open it. An untested backup is a guess.

The 3-2-1 rule

3

Copies of your data

The original plus two backups.

2

Kinds of storage

For example an internal disk and an external drive, so one type of failure cannot take both.

1

Copy off-site

Cloud or a drive stored in another building.

Words you will meet

Sync
Mirrors changes between devices. A deletion syncs too, so sync alone is not a backup.
Incremental
Copies only what changed since the last run. Faster, smaller.
Versioning
Keeps older states of a file so you can undo an overwrite.
RPO
Recovery point objective. The most recent data you can afford to lose, such as one day.
RTO
Recovery time objective. How long you can wait to be working again.
Air gap
A copy with no live connection to your computer or network.

Practical tips

Name the restore test

Put a repeating reminder in your calendar called "Restore one file". Without it, testing never happens.

Lock the cloud account

Use a unique password and a second sign-in step. Anyone who gets into the account can delete the backup too.

Unplug after backing up

An external drive left connected can be encrypted along with the computer. Disconnect it when the run finishes.

Label the drive

Write the contents and the date of the last backup on it. Rotate two drives if the data matters a lot.

Encrypt portable copies

A drive that travels can be lost. Turn on disk encryption so a lost drive is not a leaked one.

Watch for failed runs

Check the backup tool's status page monthly. Automatic backups fail quietly when a drive is full or an account expires.

About Project

Method and conclusion

Methodology

  1. Review common practiceSummarise the methods people use and the guidance usually given: 3-2-1, automation, off-site copies, restore tests.
  2. Collect responsesA Google Form asks about frequency, method, protected data, and barriers.
  3. Chart the answersResponse counts go into the Survey & Graphs page to show where habits are strong or weak.
  4. Write the guideThe Backup Guide and checklist target the weakest points found.
Limits

A self-reported survey shows what people say they do, not what they do. Respondents who join through a shared link may care more about backups than the average person, so results are best read as a sample of that group.

Conclusion

Good backup habits come down to three things: more than one copy, at least one kept away from the original, and a restore that has been tried. The guide and checklist are built around those three. Survey results, once collected, show which of them people skip most often.
